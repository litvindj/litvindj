---
title: "What I Would Tell Myself Before My First Event — Knowing What I Know Now"
date: "2026-09-30"
description: "Not a list of tips. A direct letter to the person who was about to play his first professional event and had no idea what he was walking into."
---

You're about to play your first real event. You've practiced, you know the tracks, you've got the setup sorted. You feel ready, and you're terrified, and both of those things are completely accurate assessments of the situation.

Here's what I'd tell you.

## The gear will fail at some point

Not tonight, probably. But eventually. A cable will die mid-set. A laptop will freeze at the worst possible moment. A DI box will hum in a way that makes no sense until you trace it to a power conditioner you didn't bring.

The technical failures are not the problem. Your reaction to them is the only thing that matters. The DJ who stops, calmly traces the issue, and fixes it in thirty seconds — or routes around it in ten — looks completely different to the crowd than the DJ who visibly panics. One of them looks like a professional dealing with a normal situation. The other one confirms every fear the client had about whether this was a good idea.

Get backup cables. Get a backup audio interface. Get comfortable with the idea that something will fail, because the comfort doesn't come from gear — it comes from preparation.

## The brief is not the event

You'll get a brief that describes the audience, the music style, the flow of the evening. You'll prepare against it carefully. And then you'll arrive and realize that the actual room is different from the brief in ways that matter.

The brief is what the client hopes for. The room is what you actually have. The skill is reading the gap between those two things as quickly as possible — within the first fifteen minutes, ideally — and adapting.

This doesn't mean ignoring the brief. It means understanding that it's a starting point, not a script. Every room has its own logic, and your job is to find it.

## The crowd doesn't owe you anything

This one takes a while to land. When you play something you love and the floor empties, the instinct is to feel personally rejected. Like the crowd has made a judgment about your taste or your skill.

They haven't. They just responded honestly to what was in front of them. That's useful information. They're not being unfair — they're being the clearest feedback mechanism you'll ever work with.

The sooner you can receive that feedback without defensiveness, the faster you'll improve. The DJs who take empty floors personally spend years making the same mistakes. The ones who get curious about it figure things out faster than any amount of practice will teach them.

## You will play better than you think and worse than you think on the same night

Your mental model of how the set went will not match reality. You'll think a transition was rough when the crowd didn't notice. You'll think a section was brilliant when the room was actually drifting. The subjective experience of playing and the objective result are different things, and you won't always be able to tell which is which in real time.

This is why you pay attention to the actual signals — not to how you feel about what you're doing, but to what the room is doing. The floor doesn't lie. Your self-assessment often does.

## Not every night will be great. Most nights won't be great.

This isn't pessimism. It's useful arithmetic.

Most events are not the ones where everything clicks. Most events are competent, professionally managed, reasonably well-received evenings where you did your job and went home. The transcendent nights — the ones where the room lifts and something happens that you couldn't have planned — are real, but they're not the baseline.

If you make your peace with good work being enough, you'll last. If you spend your career chasing the transcendent night and feeling like you failed every time you don't hit it, you'll burn out.

The job is to show up, read the room, do the work, go home. Most of the time, that's what it is.

## The clients who don't seem impressed are sometimes the ones who rebook

You'll finish nights feeling like the client barely noticed you. No feedback, no specific comment about the music, just a polite thank-you and a handshake. And you'll feel invisible.

Some of those clients will book you again. And again. Not because they didn't notice — but because everything worked exactly as it should have, and they have no reason to single out any one element of the evening for specific praise. The music was right, and so they didn't think about it. That's the goal.

Learn to recognize the difference between clients who didn't care and clients who cared deeply enough to trust the whole thing to you and not feel the need to manage it.

## Most of what you're anxious about doesn't matter

The specific tracks you chose tonight. Whether your EQing was precise enough. Whether the other DJ at the event noticed that transition. Whether the client's assistant was texting during your best hour.

None of that matters in the way you think it does. What matters, at the end of every single event, is whether the people in the room had a good time and whether they felt well-served by the music. That's the whole job.

Everything else — the technical precision, the library depth, the creative choices you're proud of — those are in service of that one outcome. They're means, not ends. When you confuse them for ends, you start optimizing for the wrong things.

## Last thing

After five hundred events, I still feel something before I play. Not fear exactly, but awareness. An alertness to the fact that there are real people in the room who are trusting that the music will be right.

That feeling is not something to eliminate. It's useful. It means you still care whether the night is good. The day it stops mattering is the day you should stop doing it.

Take care of that feeling. It's one of the few things in this job that will still be there after everything else has changed.
