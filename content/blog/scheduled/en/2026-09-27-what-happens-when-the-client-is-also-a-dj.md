---
title: "What Happens When The Client Is Also A DJ"
date: "2026-09-27"
description: "Having a DJ as a client is a specific challenge. They know enough to have opinions — and just enough to make things complicated."
---

It doesn't happen often, but when it does, you know immediately. The questions during the first call are different. The vocabulary is different. The requests are more specific and, sometimes, more problematic than what a typical client would ask for.

When a DJ hires a DJ, the dynamic shifts in ways that aren't always obvious until you're in the middle of them.

## The Knowledge Gap Is Narrower — and That's Not Always Good

With most clients, there's a natural division of expertise. They know what they want the event to feel like. You know how to get there. The conversation operates in that space.

A DJ client closes part of that gap. They have opinions about setups, about mixing style, about specific tracks. They know enough to evaluate some of what you're doing, which can make the relationship more collaborative. But it can also make it significantly harder.

The client who knows a little about DJing sometimes has specific preferences that work in one context and are wrong for another. They might love a particular style of mixing that's perfect for a club night and completely wrong for a corporate gala. They know they love it. They're less sure about whether it fits here.

This is where being direct is essential. Not confrontational — direct. "I understand why you like that approach, and here's why I'd do something different for this room."

## The Unsolicited Feedback Problem

Most clients observe the DJ from across the room. A DJ client stands closer. Sometimes they come by the setup and comment. Sometimes those comments are useful — context you didn't have about a specific person in the room, or a song the crowd would connect with. Sometimes they're just a DJ watching another DJ and having opinions.

The challenge is reading which kind of input you're receiving. A client who offers genuine contextual information ("half the people at table four worked together at the last company and that era of music is significant to them") is helping. A client who's watching your transitions and offering technical feedback in real time is a different situation.

I've had both. The second kind requires a moment of clear, calm boundary-setting. Politely, professionally, but clearly: this is your event and I'm here to serve it, and the best way I can do that is to focus on the room rather than on the conversation happening at the setup.

## The Temptation to Perform for Them Instead of the Room

This is the trap I've had to consciously avoid. When a DJ is in the room, especially if you know them professionally or have any sense of being evaluated, the temptation is to make choices that demonstrate your skills to someone who can understand them.

A particularly clean transition that only a DJ would fully appreciate. A more obscure track to show the range of your library. A technical choice that reads as impressive to someone who knows what they're looking at.

These choices serve the wrong audience. The other three hundred people at the event don't need to see you demonstrate your technical range. They need a room that feels right and music that keeps them present in the evening. When you're playing for the DJ client's approval rather than the room's experience, you've already lost the thread.

## When It Actually Works Well

The best version of having a DJ as a client is when they've hired you specifically because they trust your judgment more than their own for this format. They know club nights. They don't know corporate events, or weddings, or the specific culture of a Volkswagen gala dinner. They've recognized the limits of their own expertise and sought yours.

Those clients are genuinely great to work with. They understand what you're doing and why, they appreciate choices that a regular client might not even notice, and they give you the kind of feedback afterward that's actually useful for developing your craft.

I've had a few of those. They tend to become long-term relationships, because the respect goes both ways.

## What It Teaches You About Your Own Work

Playing for a DJ client forces a kind of accountability that other events don't. You can't hide mediocre choices behind a client who doesn't know what to look for. Every technical decision is potentially visible to someone who knows what decisions were available.

It's uncomfortable, and I think that discomfort is useful. The standard you hold yourself to in front of a knowledgeable audience should be the same standard you hold yourself to every night. If you're making different choices because someone who knows the craft is watching, that's worth examining.

The best DJs I know play the same way whether anyone is evaluating them or not. That consistency is what the craft looks like when it becomes genuinely internalized.
