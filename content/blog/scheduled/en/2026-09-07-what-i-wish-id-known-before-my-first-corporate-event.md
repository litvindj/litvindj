My first corporate event was a product launch for a tech company. About three hundred people, open bar, dinner service, then dancing. I'd done weddings, I'd done private parties, I felt prepared. I was not prepared.

Not because anything went catastrophically wrong — it didn't. But I spent the first half of that evening getting things consistently slightly off in ways I didn't understand until later. The gaps between what I thought I knew and what that room actually needed were specific and instructive.

Here's what I wish someone had told me.

## The Brief Isn't the Brief

The written brief said: "upbeat, modern, crowd-pleasing." Every corporate brief says something like that. It means almost nothing. What it doesn't tell you is the real demographic of the room, the three executives whose musical tastes will dominate the energy conversation, the company's cultural identity, whether people are there because they want to be or because attendance was strongly suggested.

The brief is a starting point for a conversation. The actual brief lives in the details — who the company is, who the attendees are, what the event is celebrating or launching, and what the client is anxious about. The last one matters most. If a marketing director is terrified that the evening will feel "too clubby," that fear will shape everything. Find out what the client is worried about before you plan anything.

Learn to ask questions that get past the surface. Not "what kind of music do you like?" but "what would make this a disaster?" The answers to that question are infinitely more useful.

## Background Music Is Not Background

Corporate events typically have several phases: arrival and cocktails, dinner service, possibly speeches, then the open dancing segment. The first two are often called "background music." Treat that framing with suspicion.

Background music at a corporate event is not wallpaper. It's the sound of the first forty minutes of the evening — the period when people are forming their impressions of the event, deciding how they feel about being here, and settling into conversations. The music needs to be present enough to create atmosphere without demanding attention. Too loud and people can't talk; too quiet and the room feels dead.

Getting this balance right is harder than playing to a full dance floor. At least with a dance floor, the crowd tells you directly whether it's working. Background music gives you almost no feedback. You have to be right without knowing whether you're right.

## Corporate Crowds Dance Differently

Or rather, they don't dance until specific conditions are met. A nightclub crowd is pre-selected — people came to dance. A corporate crowd is not pre-selected for anything except professional association. They're also acutely aware of being seen by their colleagues and, often, their managers.

The consequence is a delayed start. The first person to dance at a corporate event is making a social calculation, not just responding to music. They're deciding whether it's safe to dance in front of their boss. This means you need to hold energy slightly longer than you would at a club, build more gradually, and be patient. The floor will open — but on its own schedule, not yours.

It also means you cannot play what you'd play at a club, even in the open dancing segment. Not because the music is wrong, but because the social context is wrong. Tracks that are too aggressively dancey signal to a corporate crowd: "this is the kind of music people dance to in nightclubs." And a significant portion of that room does not want to look like they dance in nightclubs in front of their line manager.

## Nobody Told Me About the MC Problem

At corporate events, there's often an MC or host who will periodically interrupt the music for announcements, prizes, or speeches. The timing of these interruptions is almost never exactly as scheduled. The music needs to be dropped at a moment's notice, then restarted — and the restart needs to feel natural rather than like a record being turned back on.

I didn't know to prepare for this. I wasn't ready for the moment when the MC grabbed the microphone mid-track during what I thought was the peak of the evening. I got through it, but I made it obvious that I hadn't expected it.

Now I build explicit transition points into corporate sets — moments where fading out would feel natural — and I have a short instrumental or ambient piece ready that can be brought in immediately after a speech and then built out of seamlessly.

Small things. But nobody tells you about them until you've already learned the hard way.
