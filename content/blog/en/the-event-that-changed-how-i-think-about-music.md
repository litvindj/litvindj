---
title: "The Event That Changed How I Think About Music"
date: "2026-08-31"
description: "One corporate gala in Kraków rewired how I understand the relationship between a room and a playlist."
---

There was a gala in Kraków — pharmaceutical industry, about 400 guests, venue with ceilings so high the sound disappeared into them. My job, as stated in the brief, was "elegant background music transitioning to dancing." Standard. I'd done it a hundred times.

I had a playlist I trusted. Curated over months. Tested at similar events. I opened the night with it and stood back.

Within twenty minutes I knew something was wrong.

## The Room Was Telling Me Something I Wasn't Hearing

People weren't bad. They were talking, drinking, doing what guests do. But the energy was flat in a specific way — not bored, not unhappy, just absent. Like the music wasn't there. I watched a woman tap her fingers once on the table and then stop. I watched a group at the back gradually drift toward the bar, away from the floor.

I kept playing what the playlist told me to play.

By the time dinner ended and the dancing portion was supposed to begin, I had maybe forty people willing to step onto the floor. For a room of four hundred, that's a failure, even if nobody calls it that.

I pushed through the night with mechanics and muscle memory and it ended fine. Awards were given. People danced eventually. The client thanked me at the end.

But I drove home knowing I'd misread the room for two hours before I corrected course.

## What I Got Wrong

The brief said "elegant background music." I interpreted that as "classical, jazz, ambient." But this particular room — these particular people — were pharmaceutical executives. They were used to conference rooms. They'd been in a seminar since nine in the morning. What they needed wasn't elegance. They needed permission to exhale.

I had been playing for a fictional version of the event rather than the actual one in front of me.

The music I chose was technically correct and humanly wrong. It matched the description on paper. It missed the people in the chairs.

## What Changed After That Night

I stopped building playlists and started building frameworks. A playlist is a fixed answer. A framework is a set of questions: What energy does this room have right now? Where do these people need to go in the next thirty minutes? What's the gap between those two points?

I started arriving earlier. Not to check equipment — the equipment is always checked. I arrive early now to watch the room fill. To notice what people are wearing, how they're moving, whether they're loud or quiet, whether the loudness is excitement or nerves.

I started paying attention to what music plays in the elevator on the way up to the venue.

Small things. But they're the difference between playing music at an event and reading a room through music.

## The Deeper Shift

Before that Kraków gala, I thought about music as the thing I brought to the event. After it, I understood that music is a response. You're not delivering a product. You're listening to a room and answering back. The playlist is only as good as your ability to hear what's already happening.

I still make playlists. Still spend hours on them. But now they're starting points, not scripts.

And I never again play for the brief when the room is telling me something different.
