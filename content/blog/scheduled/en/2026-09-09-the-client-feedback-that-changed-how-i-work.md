---
title: "The Client Feedback That Changed How I Work"
date: "2026-09-09"
description: "A single observation from a client after an event permanently shifted how I approach preparation. It wasn't a complaint. That made it harder to dismiss."
---

The feedback came from the event director of a large pharmaceutical company, the morning after an annual gala I'd played. The event had gone well — full dance floor in the final hour, positive energy throughout, no technical issues. She wasn't calling to complain.

She said: "The music was great. But we noticed that the first forty-five minutes felt a bit like you were finding your feet."

I thanked her and ended the call. Then I sat with that sentence for about three days.

## Why It Was Impossible to Dismiss

A complaint is easy to handle. You can analyse whether it was fair, decide how much weight to give it, and move on. A compliment is easy too — pleasant but not actionable. What she said was neither.

She was right. The first forty-five minutes of that event had been uncertain because the crowd was different from what I'd expected. The brief had described "mixed demographics, primarily 35-50." The room that arrived was younger, more urban-leaning, significantly less interested in the classic commercial floor-fillers I'd weighted toward the start of the set.

I'd adapted. By the time the dancing started, I had a clear read on the room and everything that followed worked. But the first forty-five minutes — the welcome, the cocktail, the dinner — had been a period of audible adjustment.

What she noticed was that the evening had two different DJs: the uncertain one at the beginning, and the good one from about 9:30 onwards.

## What I Changed

The specific failure was in preparation assumptions. I had built my opening set around the brief rather than around what the brief might be wrong about.

After that conversation I changed the way I approach the opening segment of corporate events. Instead of planning a specific track sequence for the first forty-five minutes, I now prepare three different openings of roughly equal length, each calibrated to a different demographic profile. Which one I deploy is a decision I make in the first ten minutes, based on observation rather than preparation.

This sounds like more work. It is, until it becomes habit. After a year of doing it, the three-opening structure has become automatic, and the decision about which to deploy takes less time than the old method of trying to adapt a single plan on the fly.

## The Deeper Change

The structural change was useful. But the deeper change was in how I think about the pre-dancing phase of an event.

I had been treating the first hour as a lower-stakes period — warm-up, background, laying the groundwork for later. The feedback reframed it. For the people in that room, the first hour is the event. It's when they form their impression of the evening, decide how they feel about being there, settle into conversations. The dancing segment at the end is the part they might talk about tomorrow. The dinner with good music is the part they actually experienced for two hours.

Getting the first hour right is harder than getting the dancing right because the feedback loop is almost nonexistent. You can't tell if background music is working the way you can tell if a dance floor is working. The only real data point is whether something feels off — and by the time you notice it feels off, you're already forty minutes in.

## What I Tell Clients Now

That conversation changed how I brief clients, too. Before events, I now ask specifically about the demographic split between the expected brief and reality — who actually shows up versus who they think will show up. I ask whether there are specific groups within the company that have a strong cultural identity, because those groups tend to dominate the early energy of a corporate event.

I also tell clients honestly that the first forty-five minutes is the hardest part to get right, and that it requires more information, not less. A detailed brief for the opening hour is worth more than a general sense of the whole event.

She wasn't complaining. She gave me one of the most useful pieces of professional feedback I've received. That combination — positive outcome, genuine observation, something I couldn't argue with — is the kind that actually changes how you work.
