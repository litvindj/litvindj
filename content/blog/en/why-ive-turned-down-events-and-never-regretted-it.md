title: "Why I've Turned Down Events — And Never Regretted It"

---

Early in a career, turning down paid work feels like self-sabotage. You're building a reputation, filling a calendar, proving that you can sustain this thing. Every booking matters. Every "no" is money left on the table and a potential relationship that never develops.

That logic has its place. But it also has a shelf life.

At some point — and I can't tell you exactly when this happened for me — I started to understand that not every booking is worth taking. And that the clearest signal of professional maturity isn't how many events you play, but which ones you choose.

## The Events That Cost More Than They Pay

There's a category of booking that is technically profitable and practically expensive. The client who changes the brief three times before the event. The organizer who has strong opinions about every song choice but no actual understanding of how music works in a room. The event where the logistics are chaotic, the timeline keeps shifting, and by the time you're setting up you've already spent twice as much energy as the fee justifies.

I've played those events. Everyone has. And I've walked away from them feeling depleted in a way that three days of rest doesn't fully fix. Not physically tired — the other kind.

When that becomes a pattern with a specific client or a specific type of event, the equation changes. The fee pays for six hours of playing. It doesn't pay for the weeks of friction that come before it.

## Gut Feeling as Professional Intelligence

Most of the events I've turned down, I turned down because something felt wrong before I could articulate what. A conversation that went sideways in a way I couldn't exactly name. A brief that was oddly vague on the things that matter and oddly specific on the things that don't. A client who spent twenty minutes of our first call trying to get me to lower the price rather than understanding what the price included.

I've learned to trust that feeling. Not because intuition is infallible, but because after enough events, your gut is pattern-matching against a history of situations you've already lived through. When something feels off, it's usually because it resembles something that was off before.

The times I've overridden that feeling and taken a booking anyway — usually because the fee was good or because I talked myself into giving the client the benefit of the doubt — I've been wrong more often than right.

## Turning Down Events That Are Wrong for the Work

There's a second category: events where the work itself is a poor fit. Not because the client is difficult, but because what they need and what I do are genuinely misaligned.

A client who wants a human DJ as background noise while the real entertainment is something else entirely. A brief that's so tightly scripted that there's no room for any actual reading of the room — just a playlist executed in order. An event where the DJ is clearly a line item rather than a considered choice.

I can technically play those events. But the work is hollow. And a string of hollow events quietly erodes your relationship with the craft. You start showing up as a function rather than a professional. That's a bad trajectory.

Turning down events that are wrong for the work isn't about protecting your ego. It's about protecting the quality of your attention for the events where that attention actually matters.

## What Happens When You Say No

Usually, nothing dramatic. The client finds someone else. The event happens. You play something different that night, or you don't work that night and use the time for something else.

Occasionally, saying no creates a strange kind of leverage. A client who was told no, processed it, and came back with a better conversation — a clearer brief, more realistic expectations, genuine interest in what you actually do — is usually a much better client than the one who agreed to everything immediately.

I don't say no to create leverage. But I've noticed the pattern.

## The Regret Question

I said in the title that I've never regretted turning down an event. That's true in one direction: I've never finished a night and thought, I wish I hadn't played that event I turned down. The regret doesn't work that way. You don't know what you missed, because you weren't there.

What you do know is whether the work you're doing feels worth doing. That's the only scorecard that matters after the money is counted.
