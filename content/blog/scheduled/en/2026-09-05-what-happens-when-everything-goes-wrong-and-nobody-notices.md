About three years ago I was playing a corporate awards ceremony for a financial services company. Four hundred people, live presenters, a carefully timed running order with music cues for each segment. I'd prepared everything: custom edits of the intro tracks, a precise playlist synced to the event schedule, backups of backups.

Forty minutes into the event, my primary laptop froze. Not a glitch — a full, unresponsive freeze. The kind where nothing works and you know it won't be coming back.

The award presentation was ninety seconds away. A presenter was already walking toward the stage.

## The Invisible Recovery

What happened next took about twenty-five seconds and was completely invisible to the audience. I switched to my backup setup — a second laptop I always travel with, already loaded with the same files — found the correct track, adjusted the volume to match where I'd been, and cued it in. The presenter reached the podium. The music played.

Nobody noticed. The event director, standing ten metres to my right, didn't look up from her running order. She had no idea.

This is what professional disaster management actually looks like. Not heroic improvisation under pressure. Not creative problem-solving in real time. It looks like preparation so thorough that the failure has already been anticipated and the response is mechanical, not invented.

## The Failures Nobody Prepares For

Equipment failure is the easy one because it's predictable. Every experienced DJ knows a laptop can die, a CDJ can glitch, a cable can fail. The preparation is straightforward: bring redundant gear, keep backups on multiple drives, know your second setup as well as your primary.

The failures that actually hurt are the ones that aren't in the checklist.

A bride once pulled me aside during dinner service to tell me that one of the planned first dance songs — which she'd confirmed in writing three weeks earlier — was now completely off the table because it had been playing when her parents announced their divorce. She hadn't thought to mention it because she'd genuinely forgotten until that moment. I had six minutes to rebuild the emotional arc of a ten-minute sequence.

At a Volkswagen product launch, the event schedule slipped by forty minutes because the keynote speaker ran long. The cocktail hour music I'd prepared was now going to play during dinner, and the dinner set was going to play during a part of the evening that didn't exist. I had to restructure the entire musical narrative of the event in my head while the keynote was still happening, without being able to communicate with the event team who were all on stage.

## What "Containing a Disaster" Actually Means

The phrase makes it sound dramatic. It isn't. Containing a disaster means doing three things at once: fixing the problem, continuing the event as if there is no problem, and not letting your face show that there is a problem.

That last part is underestimated. A DJ booth is often visible to the room. If you look panicked, people notice. If you're staring at your setup with the expression of someone who has just seen a car crash, someone in the room will clock it and wonder what's wrong. The music might be fine. The energy in the room just shifted because of your face.

So you fix it calmly. You stay still. You look like you're doing exactly what you intended to do.

## The Preparation That Makes This Possible

I travel with more gear than I need. I keep redundant copies of every playlist. I build in transition points — moments where a brief pause or a smooth instrumental would be acceptable — so that if I need thirty seconds to sort something, those thirty seconds have a cover story.

I also run through failure scenarios before every event. Not obsessively — just five minutes in the car on the way. What if the main system goes down? What's my first move? What if they change the schedule by thirty minutes? What's flexible, what isn't?

The answer to "what happens when everything goes wrong" is: nothing, if you've done the work. The disasters happen. The room just doesn't know about them.
